The person

An essay on meaning and constraints

Everyone loves their friends.

Well, not everyone. But those who do rarely need a compelling argument for it. It makes perfect sense.

And by perfect sense, we mean something rather extraordinary: a thought can travel from one mind to another and arrive with remarkably little damage.

A made-up word. An unmistakable gesture. That unfortunate nickname from fifteen years ago.

Abracadabra.

There is a certain magic to friendship. Not because words can create things simply by being uttered. That would be magic indeed. Or worse, a curse.

But because, between two people who share enough history, a handful of words can carry an entire world.

Common sense, perhaps, is simply what you never have to explain to your best friend.

And the world would be a utopia if everyone were our best friend.

Unfortunately, there are several billion administrative obstacles to this arrangement.

We begin to notice these obstacles somewhere between the schoolyard and university.

We ask questions about power. We wonder why perfectly reasonable individuals can organize themselves into perfectly unreasonable institutions.

And eventually, we stumble upon a rather inconvenient problem: meaning.

Meaning does not exist independently of language, nor does it belong entirely to whoever speaks. It emerges through distinctions, structures, and the spaces we share with others.

We do not own the rules of language.

Rather inconveniently, they own a considerable part of us.

And the further we move from our intimate relationships, the thinner those shared spaces become.

Your neighbour may know your name, your occupation, even your daily struggles. But a sentence that would make your closest friend understand you instantly might leave your neighbour wondering whether you require assistance.

The spell no longer works. The shared world that gave it its power is no longer there.

Now imagine organizing millions of people under these conditions.

Enter the people. The state. The law. The nation. And power as the means of holding it all together.

And so we carry on, devising categories, institutions, procedures and rules to make cooperation possible between people who do not know one another.

And we need them.

But something peculiar happens along the way.

A distinction becomes a category. A category becomes a rule. A rule becomes an institution. Eventually, the institution begins treating its own vocabulary as reality itself.

The map develops strong opinions about the territory.

And the people living in it are expected to comply.

We encounter this problem repeatedly.

Working in the judiciary, I came to realize that written rules were only part of the machinery. Beneath them operated entire networks of tacit agreements and unwritten expectations.

Later, researching modern slavery, I found myself navigating reports, risk assessments, compliance frameworks and the delicate institutional art of deciding which descriptions of reality were permissible.

Apparently, even the truth must occasionally pass through Human Resources.

Years later, designing interactive experiences in Rome, the same problem returned in a rather different costume.

A room. A story. A few objects. A carefully arranged sequence of interactions.

How do you make a complete stranger understand something without explaining everything?

How do you establish just enough structure for an experience to emerge?

And how do you preserve the possibility that someone might understand something you never intended?

Different disciplines. Different instruments.

The same underlying question:

How do we make meaning more common without distorting the very thing we are trying to communicate?

Or, to put it less politely: how do we organize people without turning them into obedient little footnotes of our own assumptions?

There is a field in which these questions can be explored with unusual precision.

Game design.

A game is a peculiar arrangement of language, rules, incentives and constraints. It establishes a shared space, gives people something to do inside it, and allows meaning to emerge through their decisions.

You cannot simply declare that a player is free. You must design the conditions under which freedom becomes meaningful.

You cannot simply announce that a kingdom is unjust. You must build a world in which its injustices can be experienced, negotiated, exploited, resisted, or perhaps even defended.

And you cannot create a meaningful experience merely by enunciating one.

The spell requires a system.

For someone who had spent years studying power, writing, drawing, and occasionally wondering whether civilization was an elaborate misunderstanding, this was an irresistible opportunity.

So I set out to build a world.

It began with a political conflict. Then came its people, territories, histories, economies, factions and competing interpretations of reality.

And then, the machinery.

A card-based RPG combining roguelite progression with equipment-driven deckbuilding. Political archetypes, reputation, exploration, procedural maps, hundreds of cards, encounters, and consequences.

The result is Kingdom of None.

A rather ambitious attempt to build a small universe in which the relationship between meaning, power and freedom can be examined without requiring anyone to read a political science dissertation.

Although, admittedly, there’s enough lore to write one.

And that is where I find myself now.

Writing. Designing. Baffled, dazzled, completely hypnotized by the sheer fact that a handful of well-written rules can make entire worlds possible.

I do not claim to have solved the problem of power, language, or human cooperation.

I have simply found a particularly interesting place to experiment with them.

And, as I write these words, I am conducting another experiment.

A sequence of sentences, arranged according to rules I did not invent, attempting to carry something from one mind into a stranger’s.

Perhaps that’s why we still call it magic.
